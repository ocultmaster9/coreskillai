What this eye test measures

This free online eye test measures distance visual acuity: the smallest letter each eye can read from a known distance. It is the same quantity an optometrist measures first with a wall chart, and it is reported in the same units, logMAR and the familiar Snellen fractions such as 6/6 or 20/20. Each eye is tested on its own, because one eye can see clearly while the other is blurred and the good eye hides the difference when both are open.

Acuity depends on the optics of the eye, which is why most low scores come from short sight, long sight or astigmatism that glasses would correct. It also depends on the retina and the brain, so a score that glasses cannot improve is a reason for a proper eye examination.

How the test works

  • Screen calibration with a card. A screen does not know its own size, so the same image can be twice as large on one device as on another. You resize a rectangle until it matches a bank card held against the screen. Bank cards, driving licences and ID cards share one size, 85.60 by 53.98 mm (ISO/IEC 7810), so this tells the test how many pixels make a millimetre.
  • A measured distance. You stand 2, 3 or 4 metres away, measured with a tape. Letter size is then calculated from the angle it makes at your eye, the way a printed chart is designed for its own distance.
  • Tumbling E letters. Each symbol is the letter E turned right, down, left or up, and you say which way its open side points. No reading skill is needed, and the same optotype is used by the validated Peek Acuity test.
  • Rows of five, one line at a time. Letters sit one letter-width apart, because neighbouring letters make reading harder and a single isolated letter would overstate your acuity. Each line is 0.1 log units smaller than the one before, the design introduced by Bailey and Lovie in 1976 and used in the ETDRS charts of eye research.
  • Letter-by-letter scoring. Every letter counts 0.02 logMAR, and testing stops after a line with three or more mistakes. This is the ETDRS method described by Ferris and colleagues in 1982, and it is more repeatable than scoring whole lines.

The letters are always black on white, whatever theme the site uses, because contrast changes acuity. Lines too small for your screen to draw sharply, or too wide to fit it, are left out, and the result tells you when that limited the measurement.

What the numbers mean

logMAR

logMAR is the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution. A letter at logMAR 0.0 is 5 minutes of arc tall, with strokes of 1 minute of arc, which is the size a typical healthy young eye can just read. Every 0.1 step is one line on a clinical chart. Lower numbers are better: −0.1 is one line sharper than 6/6 and 0.3 is three lines worse.

Snellen fractions and decimal acuity

The Snellen fraction compares your distance with the distance at which a standard eye reads the same letter. 6/12 means that at 6 metres you read what a standard eye reads at 12 metres; 20/40 is the same thing in feet. Decimal acuity divides the two numbers, so 6/12 is 0.5. The test shows all three.

Where common thresholds sit

  • 6/6 (20/20, logMAR 0.0): the conventional reference for normal distance vision. Many people with healthy eyes read smaller lines.
  • 6/12 (20/40, decimal 0.5): the minimum binocular acuity for a car or motorcycle licence across the European Union, with glasses if needed (Directive 2006/126/EC).
  • Worse than 6/12 in the better eye: the start of mild vision impairment in the WHO classification, ICD-11. Worse than 6/18 is moderate impairment and worse than 6/60 is severe.

The result also notes when the two eyes differ by two lines or more, a difference worth having an optometrist look at.

Getting an accurate result

  • Measure the distance. Guessing it is the largest source of error: standing at 2.5 metres instead of 3 makes every letter look one line larger.
  • Use a laptop or desktop screen. A phone is too narrow to show the larger lines at 3 metres.
  • Keep your distance glasses on, if you have them. The result is then your acuity as you normally see.
  • Turn the brightness up and avoid lamps or windows reflecting on the screen.
  • Cover one eye with your palm, not your fingers, and do not press on it. Pressing blurs that eye for a few minutes afterwards.
  • Guess when unsure. Guessing is part of the method: correct guesses near your limit are real information, and the stopping rule prevents lucky guesses from inflating the score.
  • Use a helper or a wireless keyboard to enter the answers, so you do not walk back and forth to the screen.

How accurate can a home test be?

Well-designed digital acuity tests agree with clinic charts on average. In the study that validated the Peek Acuity app, its results differed from a standard ETDRS chart by 0.01 logMAR on average, but individual measurements varied by up to about four lines either way, and home testing varied more than testing in the clinic (Bastawrous and colleagues, 2015). Paper Snellen charts showed a similar spread. A single home result is therefore a good indication, not a precise number: repeat the test on another day before drawing conclusions.

This test also cannot see anything a chart cannot see. It does not examine the health of the eye, measure eye pressure, test near vision for reading, colour vision or the visual field, and it cannot be used for a driving licence or a work medical.

When to see an eye care professional

See an optometrist or an ophthalmologist if your result is worse than 6/12 in either eye, if your eyes differ by two lines or more, or if your vision has changed. Regular eye examinations are recommended even when vision seems fine, because some conditions, such as glaucoma, cause no symptoms until damage is advanced. Sudden loss of vision, a curtain or shadow across your sight, flashes with new floaters, or sudden double vision need medical care the same day.

Correctable problems are common. According to the WHO World report on vision (2019), at least 2.2 billion people have a vision impairment, and in at least 1 billion of them it could have been prevented or has not yet been addressed. Unaddressed refractive error, which glasses would correct, and cataract are among the leading causes.

Frequently asked questions

Is 20/20 perfect vision?

No. 20/20 is a reference point, not the limit. Many people with healthy eyes read smaller lines than 20/20, and acuity says nothing about other parts of vision such as contrast, colour or the field of view.

Why is a bank card needed?

Without it the test cannot know the real size of the letters on your screen. Any card in the standard size works, and the calibration is remembered in your browser for next time.

Can I take the test without glasses to see my prescription?

You can measure your acuity without glasses, but acuity does not translate into a prescription. Two people with the same score can need very different lenses, so a refraction by an optometrist is still needed.
