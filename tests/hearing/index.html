What this hearing test measures

This free online hearing test checks two things, one ear at a time. First, it finds the highest pitch each ear can still hear, by playing tones from 8 kHz up to 20 kHz. Second, it finds the quietest sound each ear picks up at five pitches between 500 Hz and 8 kHz, the range that carries most of the information in speech. The result is a simple picture of your hearing range and of how your two ears compare with each other.

Human hearing is usually described as covering about 20 Hz to 20 kHz, but that upper figure applies to young ears. Hearing at the top of the range is the first to fade, which is why a high-pitch check is a useful, quick way to see where your own limit lies today.

How the test works

  • Headphones and a quiet room. Speakers mix both ears and add room echo, so they cannot test one ear on its own.
  • A left and right check. One beep plays in a single ear and you say where you heard it. This catches reversed headphones and the "mono audio" setting.
  • One volume setting. You set your volume once with a reference tone and leave it there. Every later tone is played relative to that setting.
  • Highest pitch. Tones rise from 8 kHz in steps. The test stops for each ear after two pitches in a row that you did not hear.
  • Quietest sound. At each pitch the tone gets 10 dB quieter after every "heard" and 5 dB louder after every "not heard". Your level is the quietest tone you heard twice on the way up. This is a simplified form of the method audiologists use, described by Carhart and Jerger in 1959.
  • Silent trials. A few beeps are deliberately silent. If you answer "I heard it" to silence, the result is marked as unreliable rather than scored as if nothing happened.

Every tone fades in and out smoothly. A sudden start makes a click, and a click contains many frequencies, so you could "hear" a tone that was too high or too quiet to hear on its own.

Why the highest pitch drops with age

The inner ear turns sound into nerve signals with rows of tiny hair cells along the cochlea. The cells that respond to the highest pitches sit at the entrance, where they take the most wear, and they do not grow back. Age-related hearing loss, called presbycusis, therefore starts at the top of the range and moves slowly downwards over decades. Noise exposure, some medicines and genetic factors speed it up.

A study of 645 healthy people aged 5 to 90 measured hearing up to 20 kHz and found that thresholds rise steadily with both age and frequency, with the largest differences above 12 kHz (Rodríguez Valiente and colleagues, 2014). The international standard ISO 7029 describes the same age pattern for the standard frequencies. Losing the very top pitches usually goes unnoticed in daily life, because speech sits mostly below 8 kHz.

How to read your results

Highest pitch heard

This is the top tone each ear heard at your volume setting. A lower limit than you expected can come from your age, from past noise exposure, or simply from your equipment: many headphones and nearly all phone speakers stop reproducing sound somewhere above 15 to 18 kHz. If you heard nothing above 12 kHz with either ear, first repeat the test with the volume a little higher or with different headphones.

Quietest sound heard

The chart shows, for each ear, the quietest tone you heard at each pitch. Levels are in decibels relative to your volume setting: 0 is the reference tone and −40 means a tone 40 dB quieter than that. Lower on the chart means better sensitivity. The overall height of the lines depends on your volume and your headphones, so it cannot be compared with anyone else's result.

Difference between the ears

The part that does carry meaning is the comparison between your ears, because both were tested through the same headphones at the same volume. Clinicians look more closely when the ears differ by 20 dB or more at two neighbouring frequencies, or by 15 dB or more at two frequencies between 2 and 8 kHz (Durakovic and colleagues, 2019). The test applies the same rule and tells you if your result meets it. Small differences of 5 or 10 dB are normal and often come from how the headphones sit.

What an online hearing test cannot tell you

A clinical hearing test uses calibrated equipment in a sound-treated booth and reports thresholds in dB HL, decibels compared with young adults with normal hearing. Ordinary headphones and devices are not calibrated, their output changes with the volume setting and from one pitch to another, and background noise covers the quietest tones. For those reasons this test cannot say whether your hearing is normal, cannot measure the degree of any hearing loss and cannot diagnose a cause. Use it as a curiosity check and as a prompt, not as a verdict.

See an audiologist or a doctor if you notice any of these: difficulty following conversation, especially in noise; ringing or buzzing that does not go away; a blocked feeling in one ear; or a clear difference between your ears. Sudden hearing loss, usually in one ear, needs medical attention the same day. The clinical guideline on sudden hearing loss stresses early assessment, because treatment works best when it starts soon (Chandrasekhar and colleagues, 2019).

How common hearing loss is, and how to protect your hearing

According to the World Health Organization's World Report on Hearing (2021), more than 1.5 billion people live with some degree of hearing loss, and 430 million have a level that needs rehabilitation. The report projects nearly 2.5 billion people with some hearing loss by 2050, and it estimates that more than 1 billion young people are at risk of avoidable loss from loud sound. Only about one in six people who could benefit from a hearing aid uses one.

Noise damage builds up with both loudness and time. The safe listening standard published by WHO and the International Telecommunication Union in 2019 uses a reference exposure equal to 80 dB(A) for 40 hours a week for adults, and 75 dB(A) for children. Practical habits follow from that:

  • Keep personal audio at a moderate volume and take breaks from long listening sessions.
  • Use noise-cancelling headphones in noisy places, so you do not turn the music up to cover the background.
  • Wear earplugs at concerts, clubs and noisy work, and move away from loudspeakers.
  • Have your hearing checked if a noisy event leaves your ears ringing or muffled for more than a day.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take the test with my phone's speaker?

Not usefully. The speaker plays to both ears at once, so the ear comparison is lost, and small speakers cannot reproduce the highest tones. Use headphones or earbuds.

Why do the high tones sound weak or strange?

Hearing becomes less sensitive above about 10 kHz for everyone, and headphones vary a lot at those pitches. A tone near your limit can sound faint, thin or like a hiss.

Does this test check for tinnitus?

No. Tinnitus is a sound you hear with no outside source, and it needs a clinical assessment. If you have it, tell the audiologist, because it can affect how you answer near your thresholds.
