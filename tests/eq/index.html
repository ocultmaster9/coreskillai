What emotional intelligence is

Emotional intelligence (EI or EQ) is the ability to recognise, understand and manage emotions, both your own and other people's. Psychologists Peter Salovey and John Mayer introduced the term in a 1990 paper. Daniel Goleman's 1995 bestseller made it widely known.

Researchers now distinguish two approaches:

  • Ability EI treats emotional intelligence as a skill, like reasoning, and measures it with performance tasks that have better and worse answers. The best-known example is the Mayer-Salovey-Caruso Emotional Intelligence Test (MSCEIT).
  • Trait EI treats it as a set of self-perceptions: how emotionally capable you believe you are. It is measured with self-report questionnaires (Petrides and Furnham, 2001).

This test is a self-report questionnaire organised around the four branches of the Mayer-Salovey model. It tells you how you see your own emotional skills in each area. It does not test those skills directly the way a performance test would.

The four branches

  • Perceiving emotions: noticing emotions in faces, voices, body language and art, and recognising your own feelings.
  • Using emotions: harnessing moods to support thinking, for example using a calm state for precise work or enthusiasm for creative tasks.
  • Understanding emotions: knowing how emotions arise, mix and change over time, for example how annoyance can grow into anger, or why jealousy combines fear and resentment.
  • Managing emotions: regulating your own feelings and helping others with theirs, including handling stress and conflict.

Mayer and Salovey (1997) arranged the branches from the most basic process to the most complex. Managing emotions depends on being able to perceive and understand them first.

How the test works

  • 28 statements, 7 for each branch, such as "I notice when friends are upset even when they don't say so."
  • Rate each one from Strongly disagree (1) to Strongly agree (5).
  • Some statements are worded in reverse, such as "I tend to ruminate and stay stuck in bad moods." Those are scored in the opposite direction, so agreeing with everything does not inflate your result.
  • It takes about 5 minutes.

How your score is calculated

Each branch has a raw score from 7 to 35. That raw score is shown on a scale from 60 to 140. A branch score of 100 means your answers sat at the midpoint of the scale. Scores above 100 mean you mostly agreed with the statements describing strong emotional skills, and scores below 100 mean you mostly did not. Your overall EQ is the average of the four branch scores.

These figures are not normed against a population sample. The 60–140 range looks like an IQ-style scale for readability, but it shows where your self-ratings fall on the questionnaire, not how you compare with a measured group. Result labels:

  • 130 and above: exceptional self-rated emotional skills
  • 115–129: superior
  • 105–114: above average
  • 95–104: average
  • 85–94: below average
  • Below 85: an area with room to grow

How to interpret your results

The differences between your branches are usually more useful than the overall figure. Someone might perceive emotions easily but find them hard to manage under pressure, and that points to a specific area to work on.

Keep the limits of self-report in mind. People are not always accurate judges of their own emotional skills, and self-rated EI overlaps with personality traits such as low Neuroticism and high Agreeableness. A high self-rating is encouraging, but it is not proof of skill. A low one may reflect modesty or a bad week as much as ability.

Does emotional intelligence matter?

Research supports a moderate, not magical, role. A meta-analysis by Joseph and Newman (2010) found that EI predicts job performance mainly in roles that demand a lot of emotional work, such as customer service, healthcare and leadership, and much less in other roles. Popular claims that EQ matters "more than IQ" for success go well beyond the evidence. Both matter, in different ways.

How to improve emotional intelligence

  • Name emotions precisely. Research on "emotional granularity" suggests that people who can tell apart similar feelings, such as frustrated and disappointed, regulate them more effectively.
  • Pause before reacting. A short delay between a trigger and your response gives you room to choose what to do.
  • Reframe the situation. Reinterpreting a situation, known as cognitive reappraisal, is one of the best-supported regulation strategies.
  • Ask, don't assume. Checking how someone feels is more accurate than guessing from their expression alone.
  • Get feedback. Someone you trust can tell you how you come across, which a questionnaire cannot.

Frequently asked questions

Is this an official EQ test?

No. It is a free self-report questionnaire based on the four-branch model. Official ability tests such as the MSCEIT are commercially licensed and scored against large norm samples.

Can emotional intelligence be learned?

Training programmes have shown modest improvements, particularly in emotion regulation skills. Deliberate practice with the habits above is a reasonable place to start.

Is EQ more important than IQ?

The evidence does not support that claim. Cognitive ability is the stronger general predictor of job performance. EI adds value mainly in emotionally demanding work.

Why are some statements negative?

Reverse-worded statements reduce "yes-saying" bias and make the result more accurate.

Why did my score change on a retake?

Self-ratings depend on your recent experiences and mood. Small changes are expected.
