What this career test measures

This test measures your vocational interests: the kinds of work activity you would enjoy, regardless of whether you have the training for them yet. It uses the framework of the psychologist John Holland, who grouped work interests into six broad types, known by their initials as RIASEC. Holland's model is the most widely used system in career guidance, and it is the basis of the career tools of the U.S. Department of Labor.

  • Realistic (R): practical, hands-on work with tools, machines, materials, plants or animals.
  • Investigative (I): observing, researching and solving problems with ideas, data and science.
  • Artistic (A): creating with forms, designs, sound, words or performance.
  • Social (S): teaching, helping, advising and caring for other people.
  • Enterprising (E): leading, persuading, selling and making business decisions.
  • Conventional (C): organising data, records and procedures with care and accuracy.

Holland arranged the six types on a hexagon in the order R, I, A, S, E, C. Neighbours on the hexagon, such as Investigative and Artistic, have more in common than opposites, such as Artistic and Conventional. Later research by Dale Prediger showed that the hexagon rests on two underlying dimensions: working with people versus things, and working with data versus ideas.

Where the questions come from

The 60 activities are the items of the O*NET Interest Profiler Short Form, developed for the U.S. Department of Labor and published in 2010. Each of the six areas has ten items. In the developers' samples the six scales had internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha) between .78 and .87, and scores taken about a month apart correlated at .82 on average. That is good reliability for a 60-item questionnaire, and it is why we use the published items rather than writing our own.

The activities are deliberately ordinary, such as "Repair household appliances" or "Keep inventory records", so that you can picture doing them without specialist knowledge. They are shown in the same interleaved order as the computerised version, so a run of one type never sits together.

How the test works

  • 60 activities, about 8 minutes. For each one, choose how much you would enjoy doing it, from "Strongly dislike" to "Strongly like".
  • Ignore pay, training and difficulty. The question is only whether you would like the work. Thinking about whether you are qualified mixes up interest and ability, which are different things and are best measured separately.
  • You can go back. If you tap an answer by mistake, use the Previous button and change it.

How your result is calculated

Area scores. Each answer is scored from 0 (strongly dislike) to 4 (strongly like), and the ten answers in each area are added together. Every area therefore runs from 0 to 40. This is the official scoring of the Short Form.

Your interest code. Your three highest areas, in order, form your Holland code, for example IRC or SAE. The first letter carries the most weight. If your third and fourth areas are tied, the result tells you, because either letter could then complete the code.

Career matches. O*NET researchers have rated every occupation in the U.S. economy on the same six areas, from 1 to 7. We compare the shape of your six scores with the profile of 109 occupations, using a correlation. A correlation looks at which areas are high and low relative to each other, not at the overall level, so someone who rates everything generously and someone who rates everything cautiously get fair matches. The fit percentage is that correlation: 90% means the two profiles have almost the same shape.

Preparation filter. Each occupation also carries an O*NET Job Zone, from 1 (little or no preparation) to 5 (extensive preparation, usually a graduate degree). Use the filter to see the best matches for the amount of training you are willing to take on.

How to read your results

Look at the pattern before the single letters. A clearly differentiated profile, with one or two areas well above the rest, points in a clearer direction than a flat profile where all six scores sit close together. A flat profile is not a bad result. It often means broad interests, or interests that are still forming, and the result says so when it happens.

Next, read the occupations as examples of a direction, not as a shortlist to choose from. Many occupations with a similar profile are not on the list. If your top matches are pharmacist, chemist and medical laboratory scientist, the useful message is "investigative work with a practical, careful side", and that opens far more doors than three job titles.

Finally, combine the result with what an interest test cannot see: your abilities, your values, what work is available where you live, and how much you want to earn. Each occupation in the list links to its full O*NET profile, with the daily tasks, skills, education and outlook.

Does fitting your interests really matter?

Yes, though modestly. A large review by Nye, Su, Rounds and Drasgow (2012), covering more than 60 years of studies, found that people whose interests fit their work or field of study tend to perform somewhat better and are more likely to stay. Interests also predict which fields people enter and remain in. They do not guarantee success. Skills, effort and circumstances matter as much.

Interests are also fairly stable. A review of longitudinal studies by Low and colleagues (2005) found that interests become increasingly consistent through adolescence and are quite stable from early adulthood onward. That is why an interest profile taken at 20 is still a useful guide at 30, and why it is worth taking seriously.

Limits of this test

  • It measures interest, not ability. Liking an activity does not mean you are good at it, and the reverse is also true.
  • The occupation profiles describe work in the United States. Most occupations look similar elsewhere, but titles, training routes and licensing differ by country.
  • Large group differences exist. Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009) found that, on average, men score higher on Realistic and women on Social and Artistic interests. These are averages across millions of people and say nothing about what any one person should do.
  • Results reflect how you see yourself today. If you are going through a big change, take the test again later and compare.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the official O*NET Interest Profiler?

It uses the 60 items and scoring of the official Short Form, translated, with our own presentation and career matching. The U.S. Department of Labor has not reviewed or endorsed this version. The original is free at My Next Move.

What is a Holland code?

It is the first letters of your highest interest areas, in order. Career counsellors use three letters, such as SEC, because two people with the same first letter can still want very different work.

Why does my code differ from another test I took?

Different tests use different items. Codes from two good tests usually share their first one or two letters. If only the order of two close areas differs, both tests are telling you the same thing.

Can my interests change?

They can shift, especially before your mid-twenties and after big life changes, but most people keep a recognisable profile for decades.

Should I choose a career from this list?

Use it to decide what to explore: read the O*NET profiles, talk to people who do the work, and try a course, a volunteer role or a short placement. Those steps tell you far more than any questionnaire.

Is my data stored?

No account is needed and your answers are scored in your browser. See our privacy policy for exactly what is recorded.
