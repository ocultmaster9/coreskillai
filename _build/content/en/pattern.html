What pattern recognition measures

Pattern recognition is the ability to see the structure in something new: to notice that shapes repeat, rotate, increase or alternate, and to predict what comes next. It is a core part of fluid intelligence, the reasoning you use when past knowledge doesn't help. Pattern tasks of this kind predict how well people handle unfamiliar problems at school and at work, which is why they appear in almost every modern intelligence battery.

This test is a shorter companion to our IQ test. It uses the same kind of item, a 3×3 grid of shapes with the last cell missing, so it gives a quick read of the same ability in about five minutes.

How the test works

  • 16 puzzles, six answer options each. Pick the piece that completes the grid.
  • Easiest first. Puzzles get harder as you go, from a single rule to several rules working at once.
  • A fresh set every attempt. Items are generated from rules when you start, so you will not see the same puzzle sequence twice and no answer list can circulate.
  • No words, numbers or colours. The task is the same in every language, and colour vision does not affect your score.

The rules behind the puzzles

The puzzles are built from the rule families that Carpenter, Just and Shell (1990) found to underlie Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices, the best-known non-verbal reasoning test:

  • Constant in a row: one attribute, such as shape, stays the same along each row.
  • Distribution of three: three different values each appear exactly once in every row and every column, like a small Sudoku.
  • Progression: a quantity, such as the number of shapes or their angle, changes by the same step across the row.

Easy items use one rule. Hard items combine several at once, for example shape following a distribution while count follows a progression. Carpenter and colleagues found that the main thing separating strong and weak performers was how many rules they could keep track of at the same time. That is a working memory demand as much as a reasoning one.

The wrong options are not random. They follow the error types seen in real matrix tests: copying a neighbouring cell, or getting every attribute right except one. That means you cannot find the answer just by ruling out obviously wrong shapes.

How your score is calculated

Correcting for guessing. With six options, pure guessing gets about one item in six right, so about 2.7 of 16. The test subtracts that expected amount with the standard correction formula, so only reasoning above chance counts.

Estimating a percentile. The corrected score is converted to a percentile using the same method as our IQ test, so the two results can be compared. This test has not been normed on a representative sample, and 16 items give a less precise estimate than 36. Read the percentile as an approximate position, and use the full IQ test if you want a result with a confidence interval.

Accuracy. You also see the plain percentage of puzzles you solved, so you can compare attempts directly.

How to interpret your result

  • 95th percentile and above: exceptional. You handled the multi-rule items that most people miss.
  • 80th–94th: superior. Strong, consistent rule detection.
  • 60th–79th: above average.
  • 40th–59th: average. Single-rule items solved reliably, and some multi-rule items.
  • Below 40th: below average on this attempt. Rushing, distraction and unfamiliarity with the format are common reasons.

A short test like this varies more between attempts than a long one. If you retake it, compare accuracy over two or three attempts rather than relying on one result.

How to get better at pattern puzzles

  • Check one attribute at a time. Look at shape, then number, then rotation, then position. Most mistakes come from spotting one rule and missing a second.
  • Check rows and columns. A distribution-of-three rule is only confirmed when it holds in both directions.
  • Predict before you look at the options. Decide what the missing piece should be, then find it. This stops the distractors from steering you.
  • Slow down on the late items. Accuracy matters more than speed here, and the hardest items reward careful checking.

Getting familiar with the format gives a real but limited gain. It makes you better at this kind of puzzle, and research on cognitive training suggests the improvement does not spread much to other kinds of reasoning.

Frequently asked questions

How is this different from the IQ test?

It uses the same item type but has 16 puzzles instead of 36 and no confidence interval. It's a quick check. The IQ test is the fuller measurement.

Is there a time limit?

No. Work at your own pace. Most people finish in about five minutes.

Why are some wrong answers so close to the right one?

That is deliberate. Wrong options copy a neighbouring cell or get only one attribute wrong, so the test measures reasoning rather than elimination.

Do the puzzles depend on culture or schooling?

Less than most tests. They contain no language or arithmetic, although familiarity with puzzles in general can still help a little.

Why did my score change when I retook it?

Every attempt uses new puzzles, and a short test is naturally more variable. A difference of one or two items is normal.
