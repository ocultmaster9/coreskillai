What this IQ test measures

This test measures fluid reasoning: the ability to spot the rule behind something you have never seen before and apply it. Psychologists separate this from crystallised intelligence, meaning the knowledge and vocabulary you build up through school and experience. The split goes back to Raymond Cattell and John Horn in the 1960s and still underlies most modern intelligence batteries.

Every item is a 3×3 grid of shapes with the last cell missing. You work out how the shapes change across the rows and down the columns, then pick the piece that completes the grid. Nothing depends on reading, arithmetic or general knowledge. That is why one test can work in 43 languages, and why matrix puzzles are the standard format for culture-reduced reasoning tests such as Raven's Progressive Matrices.

How the test works

  • 36 puzzles, six answer options each, 30-minute limit. Most people finish in about 20 minutes.
  • Easiest first. Items are ordered by difficulty, so the early ones feel easy and the last third is hard by design.
  • A fresh set every attempt. Puzzles are generated from rules when you start, so no answer key can circulate and nobody can memorise the order.
  • Black and white only. We also run a colour vision test, and a puzzle that relied on colour would give a lower score to the roughly 8% of men with a colour vision deficiency.

The rules come from published research, not guesswork. Carpenter, Just and Shell (1990) analysed Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices and found that almost every item uses a small set of rules. An attribute stays the same along a row. Three values each appear once in every row and column. Or a quantity, such as the number of shapes or their rotation, steps up by the same amount each time. A puzzle gets harder as more attributes follow different rules at once, and that is how the test is ordered.

How your score is calculated

Scoring takes three steps, and each one is there to keep the result honest.

1. Correcting for guessing. With six options, someone who guesses every item still gets about 6 of 36 right. That expected count is subtracted from your raw score using the standard correction formula, so lucky guesses do not count as reasoning.

2. Converting to the IQ scale. The corrected proportion is placed on the familiar scale where 100 is the average and the standard deviation is 15. About two-thirds of people fall between 85 and 115, and about 95% fall between 70 and 130. This test has not been standardised on a representative population sample. The conversion uses a stated statistical assumption, not a published norm table, so treat your figure as an estimate.

3. Reporting a range, not a single number. No test is perfectly reliable. For a matrix test of this length, the standard error of measurement is about 6 IQ points, so your result comes with a 95% confidence interval of roughly ±11 points. If your interval is 104–126, your true score most likely lies somewhere in that range. The single number in the middle is the least important part of the result.

Two limits are built in deliberately. Scores are capped at 135, because 36 items cannot reliably tell a score of 140 from 150; separating people that far above average takes many more very hard items. And if your score is no better than chance, the test tells you it could not produce an estimate, rather than printing a very low number it cannot justify.

How to interpret your result

Result bands follow the ranges used in most intelligence research:

  • 130 and above: highly gifted, about the top 2%
  • 120–129: superior, about the top 9%
  • 110–119: high average
  • 90–109: average, where about half of all people score
  • 80–89: low average
  • Below 80: below average

The percentile tells you what share of the population would be expected to score below you on this scale. It is not the share of CoreSkillAI visitors you beat. Your score on any given day also depends on how well you slept, whether you were interrupted, and how familiar the puzzle format already is to you. Each of these can move a result by several points. If you retake the test, expect some variation, and look at where your intervals overlap rather than at the highest single number.

This is a screening tool for curiosity and self-reflection. It cannot diagnose giftedness or an intellectual disability, and it should not be used for school, job or legal decisions. Those need a supervised assessment such as the WAIS-IV or the Stanford-Binet, given by a qualified psychologist.

Can you improve your IQ score?

The research gives a mixed but useful answer.

  • Education matters. A 2018 meta-analysis by Ritchie and Tucker-Drob found that each extra year of schooling raised intelligence test scores by roughly 1 to 5 points.
  • Brain-training games mostly don't transfer. Practising a specific task makes you better at that task. Large reviews, including Melby-Lervåg and Hulme (2013), found little evidence that the improvement carries over to general reasoning.
  • Practice effects are real but shallow. Knowing the matrix format typically adds a few points on a retest. That comes from familiarity, not a change in ability.
  • Your state on the day counts. Sleep, stress, illness and distraction all lower performance temporarily. For the most accurate result, take the test rested, in one sitting, somewhere quiet.

Fluid reasoning usually peaks in early adulthood and then declines slowly. Crystallised knowledge keeps growing for decades. Regular physical activity and good cardiovascular health are linked to slower cognitive decline with age.

Frequently asked questions

Is this a real IQ test?

It uses the same type of item as established non-verbal reasoning tests, and it is scored with a guessing correction and a confidence interval. It is not a clinical test, and it has not been normed on a representative sample, so the result is an estimate.

What is a good IQ score?

By definition, 100 is the average score. Anything from 90 to 109 is in the average range, 110–119 is high average, and 120 or above puts you in roughly the top 10%.

Why did I get a range instead of one number?

Every test contains measurement error. The range shows where your true score most likely falls. Tests that report a single exact number are hiding that uncertainty, not removing it.

Why is the maximum score 135?

A short test does not have enough very difficult items to separate people who are far above average. Reporting 145 or 160 from 36 puzzles would be false precision.

Can I retake the test?

Yes. You get new puzzles every time. Expect your score to move by a few points between attempts. That is normal measurement variation plus a small practice effect.

Does the test work the same in every language?

Yes. The puzzles contain no words or numbers, so the reasoning task is identical in all 43 languages. Only the instructions are translated.
