What this love language test measures

This free love language test scores the five ways of giving and receiving love that Gary Chapman described in his 1992 book The Five Love Languages: words of affirmation, quality time, receiving gifts, acts of service and physical touch. The idea is simple. People feel loved in different ways, and partners who know what matters most to each other can show love in a way that lands.

Most love language quizzes make you choose between two options again and again, so one language always wins. This test works differently: you rate every statement on its own, so you get a separate score for all five languages and can see when several matter to you about equally.

How the test works

  • 25 statements, five for each language. Each one describes a situation, such as a partner helping without being asked or giving you their full attention, and you rate how true it is for you.
  • Rating instead of forced choice. Researchers have criticised forced-choice quizzes because they invent a single winner even when a person values everything. A rating scale lets each language stand on its own.
  • Scores from 0 to 100%. Each language score is your average answer on its five statements. Your strongest language is the highest score, and any language within five points of it is shown as a joint favourite.
  • Tips for a partner. For your strongest languages you also get one practical suggestion you can share with the person you love.

If you are single, answer with a past partner in mind or think about how you would like to be treated. The test is about receiving love, which is what most people mean when they ask for their love language.

The five love languages

Words of affirmation and quality time

Words of affirmation are compliments, encouragement, thanks and simply hearing "I love you". People who score high here are often hurt more by criticism, too. Quality time means undivided attention: conversations, shared activities and time together without phones or distractions.

Gifts, acts of service and physical touch

Receiving gifts is less about money than about the thought behind a present. Acts of service are practical help, such as cooking, fixing something or taking a chore off your list. Physical touch includes hugs, holding hands, a hand on the shoulder and simply sitting close.

What the research says

Chapman built his model from his work as a counsellor, not from controlled studies, and scientists have only tested it in the last two decades. Some of the results support it. In a confirmatory factor analysis, Egbert and Polk (2006) found that five separate factors fitted people's answers better than models with fewer factors. Mostova, Stolarski and Matthews (2022) studied 100 couples and found that partners who better matched each other's preferred language reported higher relationship and sexual satisfaction.

Other findings are less flattering. Bunt and Hazelwood (2017) found that couples who shared the same love language were not more satisfied than couples who did not; how well partners managed their own behaviour mattered more. In a 2024 review, Impett, Park and Muise concluded that there is little evidence that each person has one main love language, that there are exactly five, or that matching languages is the key to a happy relationship. They suggest thinking of love as a balanced diet: most people need all of these forms of care, in different amounts at different times.

  • What holds up: people differ in which expressions of love they value most, and knowing your partner's preferences can help.
  • What is uncertain: whether everyone has a single main language and whether five categories cover every way people show love.
  • What is not supported: the idea that a relationship fails simply because two people speak different love languages.

How to use your result

Treat your scores as a conversation starter, not a label. The most useful step is to compare results with your partner and talk about specific moments when you felt most loved and least noticed. A high score tells you what fills you up; a low score does not mean that form of love is unwelcome, only that it matters less to you than the others.

Your preferences can also change with circumstances. Someone with a new baby may suddenly value acts of service far more, and partners in a long-distance relationship often value words and messages more than usual. Retaking the test after a big change in your life can show how your needs have shifted.

Tips for getting an accurate result

  • Think of real situations. Picture what actually made you feel loved, not what you think should matter.
  • Use the whole scale. Save the top answer for things that genuinely move you and the bottom one for things that leave you cold.
  • Answer for yourself. Do not answer the way your partner, friends or family would expect you to.
  • Take it with your partner. Each of you answers alone, then you compare scores and tips together.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the official 5 Love Languages quiz?

No. The 5 Love Languages® is a registered trademark of The Moody Bible Institute of Chicago, which publishes Chapman's books and official quiz. This is an independent test with its own statements and a rating scale, so your result may differ from the official one.

What if all my scores are similar?

That is a common and perfectly healthy result. It means you appreciate many kinds of affection, which fits the research view that most people value all five. Talk with your partner about which forms you would like more of right now.

Can a love language test tell whether a relationship will last?

No. Love languages describe preferences, not compatibility. Long-term relationship quality depends much more on things like kindness, trust, how couples handle conflict and how responsive partners are to each other's needs.
