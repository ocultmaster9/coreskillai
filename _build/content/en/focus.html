What this focus test measures

This test uses the Stroop task, one of the most studied experiments in psychology. It measures cognitive control, also called inhibition: your ability to ignore an automatic reaction and do what the task actually asks. That skill underlies staying on task, resisting distraction and switching off habits when they don't fit.

John Ridley Stroop described the effect in 1935. When the word "RED" is printed in blue ink, naming the ink colour is noticeably slower and more error-prone than when the word and ink match. Reading is so automatic for fluent readers that the word's meaning gets processed whether you want it to or not, and you have to override it. Colin MacLeod's (1991) review of more than half a century of Stroop research called it one of the most reliable effects in cognitive psychology.

How the test works

  • A colour word appears, printed in coloured ink. Press the button for the ink colour and ignore what the word says.
  • There are 32 trials. In 16 of them the word and ink match (congruent, e.g. "BLUE" in blue). In the other 16 they conflict (incongruent, e.g. "BLUE" in green).
  • Five colours are used, and the trials are shuffled so you cannot predict which type comes next.
  • The words appear in the language of the page you are using, because the effect depends on reading a language you know.

How your score is calculated

Many online "focus tests" simply reward fast clicking. The Stroop task measures something more specific: how much the conflict slows you down. The main result is the interference effect:

Interference = median time on incongruent trials − median time on congruent trials

  • Only correct responses count, because a wrong answer does not show how long you took to resolve the conflict.
  • Responses under 200 ms or over 3 seconds are removed as anticipations or lapses.
  • Medians are used instead of averages, so one slow trial cannot distort the result.
  • Your result is scored only if you answered at least 60% of trials correctly and there are enough clean trials of each type. Very fast responding with poor accuracy is not good cognitive control.

A smaller interference effect means stronger cognitive control: the word's meaning disrupted you less. The test converts your interference effect into an approximate percentile using a model centred on a typical effect of 120 ms, and lowers it slightly if your accuracy was below perfect. The model is a reasonable assumption for button-press Stroop tasks. It is not a norm table collected from this test.

How to interpret your result

  • Close to 0 ms: exceptional. The word barely affected you.
  • About 50–150 ms: the typical range for this kind of task.
  • Over about 250 ms: a large effect. The conflict slowed you down considerably on this attempt.

You also see your median times for both trial types and your accuracy. Look at all three together. Someone slow on every trial but with little interference has good control and just responds carefully. Someone fast on congruent trials who makes many incongruent errors is being pulled by the word.

A few factors influence the result without reflecting "focus" as such. Reading fluency in the page's language makes the effect bigger, because the word is harder to ignore. Being tired or distracted usually increases both errors and interference. Unfamiliarity with the button layout slows the first few trials, which is one reason the test uses 32 of them.

Can you improve your focus?

Repeating the Stroop task makes the interference smaller as you learn to anticipate the conflict. That improvement is mostly specific to this task. What reliably improves everyday focus is simpler:

  • Sleep. Sleep loss weakens inhibitory control noticeably.
  • Remove competing input. Notifications and background speech draw on the same control system the Stroop task measures.
  • Single-task. Switching between tasks has a measurable time cost each time.
  • Take breaks. Sustained attention drops over long, unbroken sessions. Short breaks help you recover.
  • Exercise. Regular aerobic activity is associated with better executive function, especially in older adults.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal Stroop effect?

In button-press versions like this one, a slowdown of roughly 50 to 150 ms on conflicting trials is common. The exact value depends on the setup.

Why am I slower when the word doesn't match?

Because reading is automatic. Your brain processes the word's meaning even though the task tells you to ignore it, and overriding that takes time.

Is this test useful for ADHD?

Stroop tasks are used in research on attention, but a single online result cannot diagnose or rule out ADHD. Please talk to a qualified professional if you have concerns.

Why does it use my language?

The effect depends on reading. A colour word in a language you don't read produces little or no interference, which would make the test meaningless.

Why was my result not scored?

If too many answers were wrong or too few clean trials remained, there is not enough reliable data to calculate interference. Try again at a steady pace, aiming for accuracy.
