What this colour vision test measures

This test measures hue discrimination: how well you can tell apart colours that differ only slightly in hue, and put them in order. It is based on the hue arrangement method developed by Dean Farnsworth in the 1940s, used in the Farnsworth-Munsell 100 Hue Test. That test is still one of the standard clinical tools for assessing colour vision.

Unlike plate tests such as the Ishihara, where you read a number hidden in dots, arrangement tests don't only ask whether you can see a difference. They show where along the colour circle your discrimination is weakest.

How the test works

  • There are 4 rows, each covering one section of the colour circle: red to yellow, yellow to green, green to blue, and blue back to red.
  • Each row has two fixed anchor colours at the ends and 8 movable chips in between, shuffled at random.
  • Drag the chips into a smooth progression from one anchor to the other.
  • When you finish, each row is compared with the correct order.

How your score is calculated

For each chip, the test measures how many positions it sits from its correct place and adds up these distances across all four rows. That gives your total error score. A perfect arrangement scores 0. Swapping two neighbouring chips adds 2. Larger misplacements add more.

The total error is then converted to an approximate percentile, where a lower error means a higher percentile, and a result band:

  • 0: perfect arrangement
  • 1–8: excellent hue discrimination
  • 9–18: good; small swaps between very similar chips are common in people with normal colour vision
  • 19–32: average
  • 33–50: possible minor deficiency
  • Over 50: possible significant deficiency

Look at which rows gave you trouble as well as your total. Errors concentrated in the red-yellow-green rows fit the pattern of red-green deficiencies. Errors in the green-blue and blue-red rows are more typical of blue-yellow difficulties.

Types of colour vision deficiency

Normal human colour vision uses three types of cone cell, sensitive to long (L), medium (M) and short (S) wavelengths. A colour vision deficiency happens when one type is missing or its sensitivity is shifted.

  • Deuteranomaly and deuteranopia (M cones affected): the most common type. Greens and reds are easily confused.
  • Protanomaly and protanopia (L cones affected): reds look darker and are confused with greens and browns.
  • Tritanomaly and tritanopia (S cones affected): rare. Blues are confused with greens, and yellows with pinks or violets.
  • Achromatopsia: very rare total colour blindness.

Red-green deficiencies are inherited on the X chromosome, so they are far more common in men. Among people of Northern European descent, about 8% of men and 0.5% of women have a red-green deficiency. Rates are somewhat lower in many other populations (Birch, 2012). Acquired colour vision changes can also result from eye disease, some medications, or the natural yellowing of the eye's lens with age, which mainly affects blue discrimination.

Important limits of any screen-based test

A clinical hue test uses carefully manufactured caps viewed under standardised daylight lighting. On a screen, the colours you see depend on your display, its brightness, any colour temperature or night-mode settings, and the light in your room. That means:

  • Turn off night mode, blue-light filters and "true tone" style colour adjustments before starting.
  • Set your screen to a comfortable, fairly high brightness and avoid glare.
  • Results on different devices are not directly comparable.

This test is a screening and self-awareness tool. It cannot diagnose a colour vision deficiency. If your result suggests one, or colour matters for your work or studies (for example in electrical work, aviation, design or some medical fields), an optometrist can test you with calibrated tools such as the Ishihara plates, the Farnsworth D-15 or an anomaloscope.

Living with a colour vision deficiency

Most people with a mild deficiency adapt so well that they only find out in adulthood. Helpful habits include labelling clothing and cables, using apps that name colours through the phone camera, and turning on colour-blind modes in operating systems and games. Tinted "colour-blind glasses" can make some colours look more distinct to some wearers, but they do not restore normal colour vision.

Frequently asked questions

Can this test tell me if I am colour blind?

It can show signs of reduced hue discrimination, but only an in-person examination with calibrated equipment can diagnose a deficiency.

Why did I make errors if my colour vision is normal?

Neighbouring chips differ very slightly, and screens add variation. A few small swaps are common in people with normal vision.

Is colour blindness more common in men?

Yes. The genes for the red and green cone pigments are on the X chromosome, so men, who have one X chromosome, are affected far more often than women.

Can colour vision get worse over time?

Inherited deficiencies stay stable. Acquired changes can come from eye conditions, some medications and ageing of the lens. A sudden change is a reason to see an eye doctor.

Does this test affect my IQ or pattern test score?

No. Our reasoning tests are black and white on purpose, so colour vision never affects them.
