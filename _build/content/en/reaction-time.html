What this reaction time test measures

This is a simple visual reaction time test. It measures how long it takes you to notice one expected signal and respond to it with one movement. There is only one possible stimulus and one possible response, so there is no decision to make. That separates it from choice reaction time, where you have to pick between several responses and every extra option slows you down, a pattern known as Hick's law.

Reaction time is one of the oldest measurements in experimental psychology. In the 1860s the Dutch physiologist F. C. Donders used it to estimate how long mental processes take. Simple reaction time is still used in research today as a quick marker of processing speed, alertness and fatigue.

How the test works

  • 12 rounds. In each round a box waits for a random delay of about 1.6 to 4.4 seconds and then turns green. You click or tap as soon as you see the change.
  • The delay is random so you cannot predict it and start the movement early. Clicking before the box turns green shows a "too early" message and that attempt does not count.
  • Twelve rounds, not three or five. Reaction time is noisy. One lapse or one lucky anticipation can shift a short average by tens of milliseconds, which is bigger than the differences the test is trying to show.

How your score is calculated

Filtering. Responses faster than 120 ms are removed. They are anticipations that happened to land just after the change, because nobody can see, process and respond that quickly. Responses slower than 1,000 ms are also removed, because they are attention lapses rather than reaction time. If fewer than five valid rounds remain, the test tells you it cannot score the run. It does not fall back on data it has already rejected.

Median, not mean. Reaction times are right-skewed: most responses cluster together, and a few slow ones trail off far to the right. One slow response can drag an average up a lot but barely moves the median. That is why reaction-time research normally reports the median, and so does this test. Your single fastest valid response is shown too, but your median is the fairer summary.

Percentile. Your median is compared with a normal distribution centred on 261 ms with a standard deviation of 51 ms. That is in line with the medians large online click-based tests report. It is a model, not a norm table collected by us, so read the percentile as an approximate position.

What is a good reaction time?

In laboratory conditions with dedicated hardware, healthy young adults typically show simple visual reaction times of about 200–250 ms. Online tests measure more than your nervous system, though. Your monitor, browser, operating system and mouse or touchscreen all add delay. On a typical 60 Hz screen, a new frame appears only every 16.7 ms, and touchscreens and wireless mice can add further tens of milliseconds. As a result, medians of about 250–300 ms are normal for online tests, and a score around 200 ms is very fast.

  • Under 200 ms: exceptional; check that you were not anticipating the change
  • 200–240 ms: well above average
  • 240–290 ms: typical range for an online test
  • Over 300 ms: slower than typical; fatigue, a slow device or distraction are the usual causes

Because the hardware affects the number, compare your results only with other results from the same device.

What affects reaction time?

  • Age. Simple reaction time is at its fastest in the late teens and twenties and slows gradually with age. Large population studies, such as Deary, Der and Ford (2001), show the slowing is steady rather than sudden, and variability between trials increases too.
  • Sleep and fatigue. Sleep deprivation mainly increases lapses, meaning occasional very slow responses, and makes performance less consistent. The median is designed to resist those lapses, but many of them will still show.
  • Stimulants and alcohol. Caffeine can speed responses slightly in tired people. Alcohol slows them and increases errors.
  • Practice and attention. Some improvement over the first few runs is normal as you get used to the task. After that, results settle.
  • Input device. A wired mouse on a desktop computer usually produces faster results than a touchscreen or trackpad.

How to get your most accurate result

  • Use the same device each time, preferably a wired mouse or keyboard.
  • Close other tabs and apps that might make the browser stutter.
  • Rest your finger on the button so the movement itself is short.
  • Watch the box, not the cursor, and don't try to guess the timing.
  • Take the test two or three times and compare your medians, not your single best click.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average human reaction time?

For a simple visual stimulus, laboratory studies usually find about 200–250 ms in young adults. Online tests usually land between 250 and 300 ms because the device adds latency.

Why is my score slower on my phone?

Touchscreens process input in several steps, and many phones add delay between the touch and the event the browser receives. The difference is the device, not you.

Why do you show a median instead of an average?

One distracted round can add hundreds of milliseconds to an average. The median shows your typical response and is the standard summary in reaction-time research.

Can I train my reaction time?

Practice on this exact task gives modest, task-specific gains, mostly in consistency. Being well rested and alert makes a bigger difference to everyday reactions than training does.

Why was one of my clicks ignored?

Responses under 120 ms or over 1,000 ms are treated as anticipations or lapses, not real reactions, and are excluded from your score.

Is reaction time linked to intelligence?

There is a small but consistent correlation between faster, more consistent reaction times and higher scores on intelligence tests. It is far too weak to tell you anything about one person's intelligence.
