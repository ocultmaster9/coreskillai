What the Big Five measures

The Big Five, also called the five-factor model, is the most widely used framework for describing personality in scientific research. It grew out of the "lexical hypothesis": the idea that the personality differences that matter most in daily life end up as words in everyday language. When researchers analysed thousands of personality adjectives, the same five broad dimensions kept appearing, across different languages and questionnaires:

  • Openness to experience: curiosity, imagination, interest in ideas and art, and preference for variety over routine.
  • Conscientiousness: organisation, reliability, self-discipline and planning.
  • Extraversion: sociability, assertiveness, energy and seeking stimulation.
  • Agreeableness: warmth, trust, cooperation and concern for others.
  • Neuroticism: how easily you experience negative emotions such as worry, sadness and irritability. Its opposite is emotional stability.

Each trait is a continuum, not a type. You are not "an extravert" or "an introvert". You sit somewhere along the scale, and most people sit near the middle of most traits. This is the main scientific difference between the Big Five and type-based systems such as the MBTI.

How the test works

  • 50 short statements, 10 for each trait, from the International Personality Item Pool (IPIP). The IPIP is a public-domain item set developed by Lewis Goldberg and colleagues for research use.
  • For each statement, choose how well it describes you, from Strongly disagree (1) to Strongly agree (5).
  • Some statements are reverse-keyed. For example, agreeing with "I don't talk a lot" counts toward lower Extraversion. Mixing positive and negative wording reduces the effect of simply agreeing with everything.
  • It takes about 7 minutes. There are no right or wrong answers. Answer as you usually are, not as you would like to be.

How your scores are calculated

For each trait, your 10 answers are added together, after reversing the reverse-keyed items, giving a raw score between 10 and 50. That raw score is then shown as a score out of 100 with a bar, so the five traits can be compared at a glance.

It is important to be clear about what that number is. It shows where your answers sit within the possible range for that trait. It is not a comparison with a representative population sample, which this site has not collected. A 70/100 on Extraversion means your answers leaned clearly toward the extraverted end of the statements. It does not mean you are more extraverted than 70% of people.

Scores at 60 or above are described as leaning toward the high end of a trait, scores at 40 or below as leaning toward the low end, and scores in between as a balance of both.

How to interpret your results

No trait level is good or bad. Each end has advantages and costs:

  • High Openness goes with creativity and a taste for new ideas. Lower Openness goes with practicality and a preference for proven methods.
  • High Conscientiousness is one of the more consistent predictors of job performance and academic results. Lower Conscientiousness can mean more flexibility and spontaneity.
  • High Extraversion brings energy from social contact. Introversion is not shyness. It means preferring quieter settings and recharging alone.
  • High Agreeableness helps cooperation and relationships. Lower Agreeableness can mean directness and a willingness to challenge others.
  • Higher Neuroticism means feeling stress and negative emotion more strongly. It is also linked to noticing risks. Low Neuroticism means staying calm under pressure.

Your profile, meaning the combination of all five, says more than any single score. Two people with the same Extraversion score can come across very differently depending on their Agreeableness and Neuroticism.

Can personality change?

Personality is fairly stable, but not fixed. Large longitudinal studies (Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer, 2006) show that on average people become more conscientious, more agreeable and more emotionally stable across adulthood, especially between about 20 and 40. This pattern is sometimes called the "maturity principle". Life roles such as a first job or a long-term relationship seem to contribute to it. Short-term results also move a little with mood and context, so if you retake the test, expect small differences.

What the Big Five is good for, and what it isn't

The Big Five is useful for self-reflection, for understanding differences in a team or relationship, and for research. It is not a clinical tool and should not be used alone for hiring or selection. Self-report questionnaires reflect how you see yourself, which can differ from how others see you. Asking someone who knows you well to rate you too can be revealing.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the same as a professional Big Five test?

The items come from the IPIP Big-Five Factor Markers, a research instrument used in many published studies. Commercial versions such as the NEO-PI-3 are longer and are scored against large norm samples. This free version does not include population norms.

Is a score of 80/100 better than 50/100?

Not better, just further toward one end of that trait. The score shows where your answers fall within the possible range. It is not a ranking against other people.

How is the Big Five different from Myers-Briggs (MBTI)?

The MBTI sorts people into 16 types. The Big Five measures five continuous traits and has much stronger support in research on reliability and prediction.

Can I "fail" this test?

No. It describes tendencies, not abilities, and every combination of traits is normal.

Why did my result change when I took it again?

Small shifts come from mood, context and how you interpret items on the day. Large, lasting changes build up slowly over years.
