What this working memory test measures

Working memory is the small mental workspace where you hold information for a few seconds while you use it: a phone number between reading it and dialling it, or a list of directions while you walk. This test uses the forward digit span task, one of the oldest and most widely used measures of short-term verbal memory. A version of it is included in the Wechsler intelligence scales.

The best-known model of working memory comes from Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch (1974). It describes a "phonological loop" that holds sounds and words by silently rehearsing them, a "visuospatial sketchpad" for images and locations, and a "central executive" that directs attention between them. Digit span relies mainly on the phonological loop, which is why most people repeat the digits to themselves under their breath.

How the test works

  • A sequence of random digits from 1 to 9 is shown on screen for 3 seconds.
  • When it disappears, you type the digits in the same order.
  • You start with 3 digits. Each correct answer adds one more digit.
  • You get two tries at each length. If you miss a sequence, you get a new sequence of the same length. The test ends only when you miss both tries at the same length. This follows standard clinical practice, so a single slip does not cut your result short.

Your span is the longest sequence you recalled correctly.

One difference from the clinical version is worth knowing. In a supervised assessment, digits are usually read aloud at one per second. Here all the digits are shown together for a fixed 3 seconds, so long sequences give you less viewing time per digit. That makes the upper levels harder, and spans here can run slightly lower than a clinician would measure.

How your score is interpreted

In 1956, George Miller famously described short-term memory capacity as "the magical number seven, plus or minus two". Later research by Nelson Cowan (2001) suggests that when rehearsal and grouping are ruled out, the true capacity is closer to about four chunks. People reach spans of seven by grouping digits, for example remembering 4-8-2-9-1-7 as "482" and "917".

For adults, a forward digit span of about 6 to 7 is typical. The test places your span on an approximate percentile scale built around that average:

  • 10 or more digits: exceptional
  • 9 digits: superior
  • 8 digits: above average
  • 7 digits: average
  • 6 digits: slightly below average but within the normal range
  • 5 or fewer: below the typical range; tiredness and distraction are the most common reasons

These percentiles are approximate and based on typical adult performance. They are not norms collected from this test, and a single result says little about memory in everyday life.

What affects your span

  • Chunking strategy. Grouping digits into pairs or threes is the single biggest factor. It is a legitimate strategy, not cheating, and it is what good performers do.
  • Fatigue, stress and anxiety. These take up the same limited attention that working memory needs, and can lower your span by a digit or more.
  • Distraction. Background speech is especially disruptive, because it competes directly with silent rehearsal.
  • Age. Digit span grows through childhood, is stable across much of adulthood, and declines gently in later life. Backward span, where you repeat the digits in reverse order, declines more than forward span.

Can you improve working memory?

Practising digit span makes you better at digit span, mostly because you develop better chunking strategies. Whether that carries over to other abilities is one of the most studied questions in cognitive training. A meta-analysis by Melby-Lervåg and Hulme (2013) found that working memory training produced short-term gains on tasks similar to the ones trained, with little reliable transfer to reading, arithmetic or general reasoning.

In practice, the things that reliably help your working memory on any given day are simple: enough sleep, fewer interruptions, writing things down, and breaking information into meaningful groups.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal digit span?

Most adults recall between 5 and 9 digits forward, with 6 or 7 being typical.

Is it cheating to group the numbers?

No. Chunking is how memory works, and researchers expect people to do it.

Why do I only see the digits for 3 seconds?

A fixed viewing time keeps the test consistent for everyone. It also makes long sequences harder, so treat top-end results as a conservative estimate.

Is working memory the same as intelligence?

No, but they are related. Working memory capacity correlates moderately with fluid reasoning, because holding several rules in mind at once is part of solving new problems. Try our IQ test to measure reasoning directly.

Why did my span change between attempts?

Small differences of one digit are normal and come from attention, strategy and chance. Take the test two or three times and look at your typical result.

Can a low score mean a memory problem?

One online test cannot tell you that. If you have real concerns about your memory, speak to a doctor, who can arrange a proper assessment.
