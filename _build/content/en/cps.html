What this click speed test measures

This free CPS test counts how many times you can click in a fixed time and turns it into clicks per second. It is the computer version of one of the oldest tests in neuropsychology, the Finger Tapping Test, in which a person taps a key with the index finger as fast as possible for 10 seconds. Ward Halstead introduced it in 1947 and Ralph Reitan built it into the Halstead–Reitan battery, where it is still used as a simple measure of motor speed.

What makes this version different from most click counters is the comparison. Instead of an invented leaderboard, your result is compared with a published study in which 1,519 adults tapped a computer mouse button under controlled conditions.

How the test works

  • Choose a duration. 10 seconds matches the clinical test. 30 seconds also shows how much you slow down. 5 seconds is a quick warm-up but too short for a fair comparison.
  • Choose the mouse, a touch screen or the spacebar. The spacebar mode works like a spacebar counter. The reference study used a mouse button, so its comparison is only approximate for a key.
  • The clock starts with your first click. As in the clinical version, you are not timed while you get ready.
  • Clicks after the time is up are not counted, and the area locks for a moment so that a last click does not start a new run by accident.
  • Every click time is recorded, so the result shows more than a single number: your steady rate, your best single second, how even your rhythm was and how much you slowed down.

Scripted clicks sent by code rather than by a real input device are ignored. When the intervals between clicks are almost perfectly even at high speed, which a human hand does not produce, the result is flagged as a likely auto-clicker and not given a percentile.

What the numbers mean

Clicks per second

Your CPS is the number of clicks divided by the duration. In the reference study, adults tapping with the index finger of their dominant hand made on average 56.3 taps in the first 10 seconds, about 5.6 clicks per second, with a standard deviation of 9.6 taps. About two thirds of people fall between roughly 4.7 and 6.6 clicks per second.

Steady rate, best second and rhythm

The steady rate comes from the median time between clicks, which is the speed measure the study itself used, because one missed or doubled tap barely moves it. The best single second is the most clicks you made inside any one-second window. The variation between clicks shows how even your rhythm was: lower is steadier.

What the study found

  • The dominant hand is faster. Intervals between taps were about 15% shorter with the dominant hand, and the non-dominant hand was less regular.
  • Speed falls slowly with age. People aged 60 to 65 tapped about 14% more slowly than people under 45.
  • Men tapped slightly faster. Their intervals were about 8% shorter on average, with no difference in regularity.
  • Everyone slows down. Over 30 seconds the dominant hand slowed by about 5.6% and the non-dominant hand by about 9.9%.

The fastest sustained single-finger tapping in the study ran at intervals of about 130 milliseconds, close to 8 clicks per second. Results well above that almost always come from a different technique.

Jitter clicking, butterfly clicking and fair comparisons

Gamers use special techniques to click faster than one finger allows. In jitter clicking, the arm muscles are tensed so that the hand vibrates over the button. In butterfly clicking, two fingers take turns on the same button. Both can push the count far beyond normal tapping, which is why the percentile on this page only makes sense for one-finger tapping. The test tells you when your speed is above what one finger reached in the study.

The device also matters. Mice, touchpads and touch screens register clicks differently, and on a touch screen you can tap with two fingers at once. Compare yourself only with your own earlier results on the same device.

How to click faster

  • Warm up first. A few short runs loosen the hand and steady the rhythm.
  • Rest your palm and move only the finger. Lifting the whole hand adds a slower movement to every click.
  • Keep the movement small. Press just far enough for the button to register, then release.
  • Aim for an even rhythm. Rushing and then pausing loses more time than a steady pace you can keep to the end.
  • Take breaks. Long sessions of very fast clicking strain the hand and forearm. Stop if it hurts.

Is click speed a sign of anything?

For most people, no. Click speed mostly reflects practice, technique and the device. In neurology and neuropsychology, tapping tests are used alongside many other tests, and a clinician looks mainly at differences between the two hands or at changes over time, not at a single score. A slow result here does not suggest a health problem. If you notice new weakness, clumsiness or a tremor in one hand, see a doctor rather than relying on a click test.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good CPS?

For one-finger tapping, around 5 to 6 clicks per second is typical for adults, and 7 or more puts you near the top of the reference study. With jitter or butterfly techniques, much higher counts are possible, but they are not comparable with normal tapping.

Which duration should I choose?

Choose 10 seconds to compare yourself with the clinical test and the study. Choose 30 seconds to see your endurance. Use 5 seconds only to warm up.

Why does the test start counting at my first click?

So that your reaction time to a start signal does not lower your score. The clinical tapping test works the same way: the measurement is pure tapping speed.
