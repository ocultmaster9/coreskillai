What mental rotation measures

Mental rotation is the ability to picture an object and turn it in your mind to compare it with another view. It is the classic measure of spatial ability, the part of intelligence you use when reading a map, packing a car boot, assembling furniture, parking or following a diagram. Spatial ability is a distinct cognitive factor, separate from verbal and numerical reasoning, and it predicts success in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) fields even after other abilities are taken into account (Wai, Lubinski and Benbow, 2009).

Roger Shepard and Jacqueline Metzler published the famous experiment in 1971. People judged whether two drawings showed the same 3D object or its mirror image. The time they took increased almost linearly with the angle between the two views, as if they were turning a mental image at a steady speed. That finding is one of the clearest demonstrations that people can manipulate mental images much like physical objects.

How the test works

  • 20 items, six options each, 6-minute limit.
  • A target figure appears at the top. Below it are six candidates.
  • Exactly one candidate is the same figure rotated to a new angle. The other five are mirror images, the same shape flipped over, also rotated.
  • Mirror images are what make this a real rotation task. Their parts are identical to the target, so you can't find the answer by counting squares or matching details. You have to turn the figure in your mind.
  • Figures are generated fresh every attempt, and every shape is checked to make sure it has no mirror symmetry, so each item has exactly one correct answer.

The figures are flat shapes made from joined squares, rotated on the screen, rather than the 3D block figures of the original experiments. Flat figures work well on phones and small screens and still show the same angle effect.

How your score is calculated

Correcting for guessing. With six options, guessing alone gets about 3 or 4 of 20 right. The test subtracts that expected amount using the standard correction formula, the same way as our IQ test. If your score is at chance level, the test tells you it cannot place you, rather than printing a meaningless percentile.

Percentile. The corrected score is converted to an approximate percentile using a stated statistical model. This test has not been normed on a representative sample, so treat the percentile as an estimate.

Small turns vs large turns. Your results show how many items you got right when the figure was turned 90° or less, and how many when it was turned more than 90°. Most people are noticeably less accurate on large turns. That is the Shepard-Metzler effect in your own data.

How to interpret your result

  • 90th percentile and above: exceptional spatial reasoning
  • 75th–89th: superior
  • 55th–74th: above average
  • 40th–54th: average
  • Below 40th: below average on this attempt; running out of time or rushing large-angle items are common reasons

If you did well on small turns but poorly on large ones, you likely have a good strategy but need more time to rotate. If your accuracy was similar for both, you may be using an efficient strategy, such as tracking one distinctive corner of the shape.

Can spatial ability be improved?

Yes, and this is one of the better-supported findings in cognitive training. A meta-analysis of 217 studies (Uttal et al., 2013) found that spatial skills improve with practice, that the gains last, and that they transfer to spatial tasks that were not trained. Useful activities include:

  • Puzzle and construction games, including tile-rotation video games
  • Drawing objects from different viewpoints
  • Building with blocks or models, and 3D design software
  • Map reading and navigating without turn-by-turn directions

On average, studies find a gap between men and women on mental rotation tests (Voyer, Voyer and Bryden, 1995). That gap shrinks with practice and depends partly on test format and time limits. Individual differences are much larger than any group difference.

Tips for this test

  • Pick one distinctive feature of the target, such as an L-shaped end or a lone square, and find it in each option.
  • Check the feature's position relative to the rest of the shape: is it clockwise or anticlockwise? That is what tells a rotation from a mirror image.
  • Don't spend too long on one item. Six minutes is enough for 20 items at a steady pace.

Frequently asked questions

What is a mirror image in this test?

The same shape flipped over, like your left hand compared with your right. No amount of turning in the flat plane makes it match the target.

Why do large rotations feel harder?

Mental rotation takes time in proportion to the angle, much like turning a real object. Larger angles take longer and cause more errors.

Is spatial ability part of IQ?

It is one of several broad abilities that full intelligence batteries measure. Our IQ test focuses on fluid reasoning, and this test adds the spatial dimension.

Can I retake the test?

Yes. You get new figures every attempt, and improvement with practice is expected and real.
