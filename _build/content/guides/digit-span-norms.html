Digit span is the longest string of digits a person can repeat back correctly right after hearing or seeing it. It is among the oldest and most widely used tests of short-term memory, and it appears in intelligence batteries such as the Wechsler scales. This page explains what typical spans are, what changes them, and how to read your own result on our working memory test.

Typical spans for adults

For healthy adults, the typical forward digit span (repeat the digits in the order shown) is about 6 to 7, with most people between 5 and 9. The typical backward span (repeat in reverse) is shorter, about 4 to 5, because reversing the sequence needs the central executive, not only a rehearsal loop. A third version, sequencing, asks you to repeat the digits in ascending order, and is used in the WAIS-IV.

8Above average
7Average

5 or fewerBelow the typical range; fatigue and distraction are common reasons

These bands are approximate and are the ones our test uses. They are not norms collected from our test, and a clinician would look at the whole assessment and not a single digit span.

Where the "seven plus or minus two" idea comes from

George Miller's 1956 paper "The magical number seven, plus or minus two" popularised the idea that short-term memory holds roughly seven items. Nelson Cowan (2001) later argued that the real capacity limit, when rehearsal and grouping are controlled, is closer to four chunks. People reach a span of seven or more because they chunk digits, grouping them into pairs or triplets. Chunking is a legitimate strategy and is what good performers do.

What affects digit span

  • Chunking and rehearsal strategy. The single biggest influence on score.
  • Speech rate and word length. Span is limited by how much you can say, silently, in about two seconds. Languages whose digit names are short produce longer spans: speakers of Chinese tend to have longer digit spans than English speakers, who in turn tend to outperform speakers of Welsh, whose digit names take longer to say (Ellis and Hennelly, 1980; Stigler, Lee and Stevenson, 1986).
  • Presentation. Digits read aloud at one per second are not the same as digits shown on a screen for a fixed time. On our test all digits are displayed together for 3 seconds, so long sequences leave less time per digit, and spans may come out slightly lower than in a clinical setting.
  • Attention, stress and fatigue. They take up the same limited resource and can lower span by a digit or more.
  • Age. Forward span is relatively stable through adulthood, while backward span and tasks that require manipulation tend to decline more with age. Children's spans grow steadily from about 3 to 4 digits at ages 4–5 to near adult levels in the teenage years.

How the task differs across versions

  • Clinical versions read digits aloud at one per second; two trials per length; testing stops after two failures at one length.
  • Our version follows the same two-trial rule, starting at 3 digits, and adds one digit after each success. Your span is the longest sequence you recalled correctly.

What a short or long span does and does not mean

  • A span within the normal range tells you very little about everyday memory. Remembering where you left your keys or what happened last week uses different systems.
  • A very short span on one occasion is more often explained by distraction, tiredness or anxiety than by a memory problem.
  • Digit span is just one part of working memory. It mostly measures the phonological loop; spatial and executive aspects need other tasks.
  • If you are worried about your memory, talk to a doctor. An online test cannot assess it.

Frequently asked questions

Is a digit span of 5 bad?

It is below the typical adult range of 6–7, but not unusual, and it often reflects strategy, fatigue or distraction on the day. Try again rested, and group the digits.

Can I improve my digit span?

Yes, mostly through chunking and rehearsal strategies, and with practice on this specific task. Evidence that it raises general memory is weak.

Why is backward span shorter?

You have to hold the sequence and mentally reverse it, which loads the central executive in addition to the rehearsal loop.
