IQ Tests for Kids: What Parents Should Know

By Eduard-Dragos Condria · Published October 10, 2026 · 6 min read

How children are properly assessed, why an online adult-style test is not suitable for a child, and what to do if you have concerns.

Parents often search for an IQ test for their child, usually because of a teacher's comment, a sibling comparison, or curiosity about whether their child is gifted. The short answer is that a free online quiz is not the right tool. This page explains how children are properly assessed, why adult-style online tests do not suit them, and where to go if you have a real question.

Why our IQ test is not for children

The CoreSkillAI IQ test is designed for adults. Its puzzles, time limit and scoring assume adult reading of instructions and attention span, and it has not been standardised on any age group, children included. Children's cognitive development is fast and uneven: a score that is correct for a 13-year-old is meaningless for an 8-year-old because the reference group changes with age. Please do not use this test, or any other online quiz, to label a child.

How children are assessed properly

A formal assessment is carried out by a qualified psychologist, usually in a one-to-one session lasting 60 to 90 minutes or more. Common instruments include:

  • WPPSI-IV (Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence): ages about 2½ to 7.
  • WISC-V (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children): ages 6 to 16.
  • Stanford-Binet 5: ages 2 to adulthood.
  • Others such as the Kaufman scales (KABC-II) or the Differential Ability Scales, and non-verbal tests such as Raven's Coloured Progressive Matrices for younger children or those with language difficulties.

The psychologist compares the child with a norm sample of the same age, observes how the child approaches the tasks, and reports index scores (for example verbal comprehension, visual-spatial, fluid reasoning, working memory and processing speed), not only a single number.

Why a single score at young ages is shaky

IQ scores in early childhood are less stable than in adulthood. They are affected by language, attention, mood on the day, familiarity with the testing situation and fast developmental change. Professionals interpret them as a snapshot, often recommending reassessment. Putting a fixed label on a young child can do more harm than good.

Reasons to seek an assessment

  • Your child is struggling with reading, writing, maths or attention despite good teaching, and you want to understand why (for example, a possible learning difficulty or ADHD).
  • Your child seems far ahead and school asks for an assessment for a gifted programme.
  • A teacher or doctor has recommended a psychological evaluation.

Start by talking with your child's teacher or school psychologist. In many countries school-based services provide assessment, and your family doctor or paediatrician can refer you. If you go privately, check that the psychologist is qualified and registered in your country.

What matters more than an IQ number

  • Curiosity, effort and enjoyment of learning.
  • Sleep, nutrition, physical activity and a stable routine.
  • Being read to and talked with, play, and a variety of experiences.
  • A supportive relationship with teachers and caregivers.

Research on mindset and motivation suggests that how adults talk about ability matters. Praising effort and strategy supports persistence better than telling a child that they are "smart" or "not good at" a subject.

Fun activities, with realistic expectations

Puzzles, board games, building toys and mental rotation or pattern games are good for play and may build specific skills. Spatial skills in particular respond to practice (see mental rotation). But there is little evidence that games raise general intelligence, so use them because they are enjoyable.

If you are an adult looking for a score

If you are curious about your own result, take the IQ test and read how accurate online tests are first. For a reliable result you will need a professional assessment.

Frequently asked questions

At what age can a child take an IQ test?

Standard individual tests start around 2½ years, but scores before school age are less reliable. Most formal assessments happen from 5 or 6 onwards.

Can a child be taught to score higher?

Familiarity with test tasks can raise a score slightly. That is a practice effect, not a real change in ability, and it can make the result less informative.

Is a high IQ a guarantee of success?

No. It predicts some outcomes on average, but motivation, health, support and opportunity matter a great deal.
